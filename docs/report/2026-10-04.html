Where it stands

Every figure on this page is read out of the repository by tools/report-facts.sh rather than recalled. 619 tests pass, none fail, three are todo. tsc and oxlint are clean. The whole history is on main under one author; the eight claude/* branches are all zero ahead and exist only as stale pointers.

The project set out to make exercises that follow real patterns that exist, not random. That requirement is what the loop spent itself on, and it is the part that came out well. What it did not spend itself on is the microphone — which is the premise the whole app rests on, and which does not exist in any form.

What the loop actually bought

The musical assets are the result worth keeping: 37 rhythm cells, 26 named whole-bar patterns and 35 progression templates, each validated at construction and tested as data rather than one generated seed at a time. A wrong template is wrong in every key at once, so it is found once.

Two structural decisions did most of the work. Pitches are stored as a letter, an alteration and an octave rather than as MIDI numbers, which is why G♯ and A♭ stay distinct and why a C blues scale spells G♭ and G instead of F♯ and G. And roman numerals stay symbolic until the last moment, so the degree fixes the root's letter before any accidental is chosen — which is the whole reason V/V in C comes out D–F♯–A.

The generators are tested by breaking what the guards protect and watching the test go red. That repeatedly found guards that could not fail. It also found a real defect: a plagal close produced the exact V–IV retrogression the harmony generator exists to prevent, in 358 of 600 runs, and the existing repair could not fix it because both chords were locked by cadence writes.

Not built

No audio capture. The app cannot hear anything. The analysis chain that would process the sound is finished and tested off-device, and the seam it will plug into is decided (ADR 0012: judging consumes performed notes, never audio, so the same grader serves both a microphone and a MIDI keyboard). But nothing captures. Every exercise is answered by tapping a button, which is not the app that was asked for.

No melody generator. The largest single piece of the original design — contour planning, motivic development, and a beam search that makes every note record why it belongs — does not exist. Sight reading cannot be built until it does, and it is the part that decides whether generated music sounds like music or merely obeys the rules.

Neither shipping target. There is no service worker and no Android project. Both are pinned as todo tests in src/architecture.test.ts, so they fail the moment someone claims otherwise, but today the app runs in a dev server and nowhere else.

Open: the missing last note

On the Rhythms reference screen, the first press of a play button appears to drop the final note of the figure. It is reproducible for the person who reported it and has not been reproduced in a test.

Four explanations were eliminated earlier: the scheduling arithmetic was checked against every figure in every metre, cold-graph headroom was measured at 4.5 ms against 60 ms available with no note starting in the past, 92 envelopes were checked for malformation, and the master-gain stop window was ruled out.

Two candidates remained, and one of them has now been eliminated too. Every Oscillator and AudioBufferSourceNode start and stop was instrumented. A figure’s first press and its later presses are identical in every respect: same source count, same offsets to the millisecond, same stops, a 1.04 s tail margin, nothing scheduled in the past. Crucially, a figure’s own first press — when it was not the first press since the reload — scheduled exactly what its second did. There is no per-figure state that could be cold.

So of the two, this one survives:

The first press after a page reload, whatever the figure — the audio graph waking, not anything cached per figure.

That mechanism has now been tested on a real browser, and it is wrong. The proposed explanation was that the context is created suspended and resume() is asynchronous, so a bar laid out before the resume lands sits on a clock that then jumps. Measured in Chrome 126, with the click driven as a genuine user activation: at the first scheduling call after a page load the context reports running, with currentTime 0. It is not born suspended and there is no async resume. The explanation is withdrawn by the person who proposed it, rather than left to harden.

What is actually there is narrower, and does not account for the symptom. The context is created lazily on the first press; its clock then sits at 0 for 30–40 ms before advancing by one render quantum. The first note is scheduled at +60 ms, clearing that stall by only 20–30 ms. Nothing landed in the past in that run. But scheduling is identical on every press — 28 sources, +0.060 s to +2.203 s, 28 stops, a 1.04 s tail margin — and there is no setTimeout, setInterval or Date.now anywhere in src/audio or the sound module, so nothing cuts playback on wall-clock time. If the first press loses anything, the thin margin is at the front of the bar, not the back. A lost last note is not explained, and could not be reproduced.

So this defect is less understood than the previous version of this page claimed, not more. Two measurement caveats are why it is not closed either way: headless Chrome has no audio device and its clock ran about 12% slow over the window, and a null sink may open faster or slower than a real sound card — which is precisely the quantity that decides whether 60 ms of lead is enough.

That test has now been run, and it found a real defect — a different one. play() times a passage from currentTime + 0.06, and on a context still opening that clock is about to jump by however long the device takes; 200 ms is unremarkable, so the front of the bar lands behind the clock. Fixed by awaiting the resume the code already fires, rather than guessing at a larger headroom — which would only move the question to how large. Warm plays are unchanged.

The apparatus had to be corrected before it could show anything, and that is half the finding. The resumeCost knob had never been called from anywhere in src, and its resume() advanced the clock synchronously — closing the exact gap its own comment says it exists to model. The real caller does not await, so it reads the clock before the hardware starts; the fake handed back a post-start reading no browser can produce. Any test written against it would have been green and meaningless.

Deferring the schedule also re-opened a hole that had already been closed once: a passage waiting on the hardware can arrive after the stop that was meant to silence it — the "notes playing over the next screen" defect, with a wake-up in front of it. Plays and stops now take a ticket and only the newest survives the wait. That was found because the original defect's reasoning was written down in the code rather than remembered.

None of this closes the reported defect. It is the front-of-bar margin, and it bites on any platform where a context starts suspended — iOS Safari among them, which makes it worth having fixed. But Chrome creates the context already running, so that path never engages there, and Chrome playback is byte-identical after the change. The lost last note still cannot be reproduced and still has no explanation. This page should not be read as having resolved it.

Closed within the day: four of six exercise switches blanked the app

Found after this report was first published, and fixed before the day ended. Clear storage, open an exercise, press Start, then change the exercise in the dropdown — four of the six transitions crashed the whole page, in four different files, two of which are not components:

TransitionWhere
Interval → KeycrashKeyPrompt.tsx — .map of undefined
Interval → DegreecrashDegreePrompt.tsx — .length of undefined
Degree → Intervalcrashintervals.ts — intervalVoices
Degree → Keycrashkeys.ts — keyScoreSpec
Key → Intervalok
Key → Degreeok

The mechanism is confirmed in the source rather than inferred from the stack traces. PracticeScreen holds the live question in one useState, while the definition comes from the dropdown — so changing the dropdown leaves the outgoing exercise’s question in place and runs the incoming exercise’s code against it. Key happens to be a tolerant source; that looks like luck rather than design. There is no error boundary anywhere in src, so the failure takes the whole page.

It only bites when the destination has not been visited in that profile, which makes it a first-run crash on a very short path — the worst place to have one.

And there is a second instance that nothing found, because it does not throw. The session tally has no reset path either, so answering three interval questions and switching to key keeps the counter running: “7 of 9 this session” silently blends two different exercises. setSession appears exactly twice in PracticeScreen, at its declaration and at the increment, and the only effect in the file loads the progress store. That is why this became ADR 0015 rather than a bug list — repair only the loud one and the seam is still wrong.

What the seam actually failed at is worth stating precisely, because the registry promises otherwise: adding an exercise type is one import and one array entry, and that is true of the registry and false of the screen. Genericity is what the seam bought — one screen serves every type because it knows ExerciseDefinition and nothing about intervals — and the unpriced cost is that a generic screen holds state belonging to a particular type while the type changes underneath it. A seam cheap to extend but unsafe to switch between is not the seam it advertises. definition is a useMemo over the dropdown and changes at once; round is useState and does not.

Fixed. PracticeScreen split in two: the chrome keeps the heading, the type selector and the progress warnings, and everything scoped to a type moved into an ExerciseRound rendered key={definition.id}. Discard by remount rather than a reset path, on the grounds that a reset path is a list someone has to keep in step with the state it clears, and the evidence in front of us is that such lists are not kept in step. The session tally lives inside the keyed component, so it is per type by construction.

The regression test runs every ordered pair, driven through the selector as a user drives it, and asserts the strong form: arriving at a type by switching is indistinguishable from arriving at it fresh, compared against a freshly-mounted screen. It names no field, so it keeps covering whatever the sixth type adds. Checked against the unfixed code, which is the only way to know a test works — 11 of 13 cases fail, the crash sweep flags exactly the four known pairs, and the comparison test fails on all six, including the two that never crashed. That is the silent half a crash-only test would have waved through.

The boundary sits inside the chrome, so a throwing prompt costs the user the exercise and not the menu, and it names the seed. That forced three components rather than two: a boundary wrapped around ExerciseRound cannot show a seed that lives inside it, and questionScore/answerScore call into the definition during render — which is where two of the four crashes came from — so computing them above the boundary would have put the likeliest throw out of its reach.

Four things the app told the user wrongly; three are fixed

Found in the same sweep, listed by how badly each misleads rather than by effort. The first two mattered more than their size suggested, because this is an app for learning music and both of them taught something false. One of the two turned out to be much worse than its triage.

WhatWhy it matters
Circle-of-fifths countsfixed A wedge carried two spellings and one signature, so "D♭ / C♯" printed 5♭ — right for D♭, wrong for C♯, which has seven sharps. Each spelling now carries its own, stacked in the order of the names above it: B/C♭ reads 5♯/7♭, G♭/F♯ reads 6♭/6♯.
Scales drew the wrong signaturefixed Triaged as a label and it was the stave. The screen passed the picked key to the score while the notes came from the picked scale, so the two could disagree outright — A♭ minor with the scale set to Major engraved seven flats and then three naturals cancelling them, against four clean flats when the pair agreed. It now picks a tonic and derives the signature from what is drawn.
Dead clef controlfixed The home card and the exercise's own subtitle both advertised reading intervals off the staff, and there was no way to get there. Everything was built — both presentations declared, the engraving written, the prompt already suppressing playback when reading — and the settings field was missing, so none of it was reachable.
Mobile touch targetsopen Rhythms has 31 buttons at 25 px against a 44 px guideline. It ships as a PWA and an APK, so this is the primary target rather than an edge case.

Two of the three fixes came with a test that names no screen. The circle's sweeps every position rather than the three that pair today, with a guard against passing vacuously if the enumeration ever stopped producing pairs. The scales one asserts the general claim — for every tonic and every scale type, where a signature is offered the scale shown must be that key's own scale, note for note and spelling included.

The third is the one worth keeping. The guard that should have caught the unreachable reading presentation could not: it checked that every offered option is declared, then skipped any exercise with no field at all — so declaring two presentations and offering neither passed. It now checks both directions. That is the third guard this week found unable to fail, after a chroma claim nothing verified and a clock fake that closed the gap it existed to model.

Seven more are recorded and can wait: raw clef values and item ids shown to the user, a single-item readout, a silent no-op that does the right thing, a tab title, a template README and a dev-server port. Cosmetic or confusing rather than false.

Also open

The notation bundle is 377 kB gzipped and loads on the home screen. It is split into its own chunk, but the import is static, so the built index.html modulepreloads it: the split bought nothing on first load. Total JS is 465 kB gzipped against a planned 91 kB — the gap is the Bravura font, chosen deliberately, costed wrongly. Making the import dynamic would take 377 kB off the first paint for a reader who opens the home screen and stops there, and the architect has confirmed it is compatible with ADR 0003 — exactly one runtime import reaches the renderer, because the one-importer rule held. But it must not be done first: a lazy chunk that the service worker does not precache breaks the app offline, and vite-plugin-pwa is still a declared dependency that vite.config.ts never imports. Wire the PWA, then split.

Three progression templates are unreachable on the default settings path, and leading-tone-close is one of them. The decision was deferred to when the progression exercise is built, which is now the next piece of work rather than a distant one.

Three documents said the DSP layer computes chroma. It does not. Found after this report was first published, by the architect, who had written the worst of them. src/audio/dsp/ exports FFT utilities, an onset detector, YIN pitch detection and rhythm alignment; grep -rni chroma src/audio/ returns nothing. ADR 0013 and ADR 0012 both said a chord’s pitch classes were “answerable from the chroma the DSP layer already computes”, and the diagram in the ADR index drew it among the directory’s contents. All three now carry corrections, dated today: the two records by appended note, because an ADR is append-only, and the index by edit, because a living document’s job is to say what is true now rather than to archive its own mistakes.

Two things did not need correcting. ADR 0001 names chroma too, but it landed on 3 October and src/audio/dsp/ first appeared on 4 October, so its sentence cannot have been describing the layer’s contents — there was no layer. And CLAUDE.md, which is the source of all of it, still reads dsp/  pitch detection, chroma, onsets. It describes what the layer is for; each later document read it as what the layer holds. That line is the user’s to change and is deliberately left alone.

This matters beyond tidiness, and it changes the order of work. The measurement ADR 0013 wanted cannot be run at all, because there is nothing to measure. The chord exercise cannot be built on that claim. The sequence is: build a chroma feature, measure per-pitch-class recall on recorded chords both strummed and blocked, then build the exercise on whichever answer comes back — and if recall on inner voices is poor, the chord exercise declares itself MIDI-first, which ADR 0012 already permits an exercise to do.

It is also the second time: ADR 0005 corrected ADR 0002 for the same mistake, a claim about this codebase taken from the prose around it rather than from the code. So the ADR index now carries it as a standing convention — check a claim about the code against the code, not against the record that made it — written where the next record’s author will read it rather than attempted as a test, because a link checker validates links and not claims. No step in the chain looked like an invention. Each author was citing a source that was locally authoritative, which is exactly why nothing caught it.

Next

  1. Measure input latency on real devices first. ADR 0014, written today, settles the clock: one AudioContext.currentTime, input latency as a single correction at the capture boundary, calibrated rather than assumed, and the correction in force stored with each attempt. The forcing fact is that the Web Audio API exposes baseLatency and outputLatency but no input-latency property, while toleranceFor already pins the matching window at a 100 ms half-width across all of normal practice tempo. Ordinary round-trip latency is of that order, so an uncorrected offset does not degrade judging gracefully — past the window every note reads as missed and every attack as extra, and it looks like the detector failing rather than a clock. The experiment is small: play a click through the output path, capture it, take the difference, on as many target devices as can be reached. It is cheap now and it decides whether the rhythm exercise is buildable as designed.
  2. Capture, end to end, on one exercise. getUserMedia with echo cancellation, noise suppression and gain control all off, a worklet, and a note assembler feeding the seam ADR 0012 already specifies. Rhythm is the exercise to prove it on: the onset detector and the aligner are both finished, so it tests the plumbing and not the algorithms.
  3. The three exercises that need no new machinery — progression ID, scale ID, absolute note ID. This is the cheapest way to close the gap between the home screen and the brief, and it settles the leading-tone-close question as a side effect.
  4. The melody generator, built motif-first rather than bolted onto a legal-but-dull walk. Its real failure mode is blandness, which no test catches, so it needs listening as well as invariants.
  5. Sight reading, which is the first exercise that uses everything at once.
  6. The two shipping targets, taking the two todo tests green. The service worker moves ahead of the bundle split for the reason above, and the chroma measurement sits in front of the chord exercise.

The exercise-switch seam is off that list because it is done. What is left of the Rhythms defect is one test against a resumeCost above 60 ms, which decides whether the remaining suspect is the front-of-bar margin or nothing to do with the clock at all.

One cost of that fix cuts the other way and should not be lost. A prompt that throws on every render currently stops the app, which is the loudest possible signal and guarantees someone finds it. Contained behind a boundary it becomes one broken card among five working ones, and a defect that only breaks the exercise nobody opened this week can survive a release. So the boundary has to report and not merely display — and since nothing in this app reports anything, that means the seed on screen and in the console. Generation is reproducible from seed and settings, so a crash that names its seed is a defect someone can reproduce exactly rather than a story about a blank page.
